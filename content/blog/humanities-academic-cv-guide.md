Humanities academic CVs differ fundamentally from STEM and social science CVs in structure, emphasis, and formatting conventions. Monographs carry more weight than journal articles. Conference presentations signal active scholarly engagement. Language skills and archival research experience require dedicated sections. For comparison, see the [STEM academic CV guide](/blog/stem-academic-cv-science-engineering) and [social sciences CV guide](/blog/social-sciences-academic-cv-guide). This guide covers discipline-specific conventions for scholars in literature, philosophy, history, classics, and related humanities fields.

## How Do Humanities CVs Differ from STEM and Social Science CVs?

Humanities CVs prioritize monographs, edited volumes, and peer-reviewed articles — in that order of importance. A single monograph published with a university press often outweighs 5 journal articles in hiring and tenure decisions. For guidance on presenting publications, see [how to list publications on your academic CV](/blog/list-publications-academic-cv). Conference presentations receive more space and detail than in STEM fields because humanities scholarship develops through oral presentation and scholarly exchange before reaching publication. For formatting standards, see [how to list conference presentations on your academic CV](/blog/conference-presentations-academic-cv).

The physical length of humanities CVs also differs. While a senior STEM researcher may present a 20-page CV, a senior humanities scholar's CV commonly reaches 25 to 35 pages. This length reflects the narrative detail expected in project descriptions, the comprehensive listing of conference presentations, and the inclusion of works in progress that demonstrate an active research pipeline. Humanities search committees expect a complete scholarly record, not a curated selection of highlights.

## How Should You Format Publications on a Humanities CV?

Humanities publications use 3 hierarchical categories: books (monographs and edited volumes), peer-reviewed articles, and book chapters. Place books first, followed by articles, then chapters. Within each category, use reverse chronological order. Format book entries with full bibliographic information: author name, title in italics, publisher, and year. For example: "Smith, Jane A. *The Rhetoric of Reform: Victorian Literature and Social Change*. Cambridge University Press, 2024."

Peer-reviewed articles use MLA or Chicago format depending on your discipline. Literature and language fields use MLA. History, philosophy, and classics use Chicago. Include volume, issue, and page numbers for all articles. For works in progress, use clear status labels: "Under contract with Oxford University Press," "Under review at *PMLA*," "In preparation." Search committees understand that humanities publishing timelines span years and value evidence of an active pipeline even when works have not yet appeared in print.

## How Should You Present Conference Presentations and Invited Talks?

Humanities CVs distinguish between conference presentations and invited talks with separate sections. Invited talks — presentations at other universities, research seminars, or scholarly societies where you were specifically invited — carry significantly more weight than conference presentations. Place invited talks in their own section above conference presentations.

Conference presentations include the paper title, conference name, location, and date. For major conferences in your field — the Modern Language Association convention for literature scholars, the American Historical Association meeting for historians — the conference name alone signals the venue's prestige. For presentations that developed into publications, add a note: "This paper formed the basis of chapter 3 of *The Rhetoric of Reform* (Cambridge, 2024)." This note connects your presentation record to your publication record, reinforcing the narrative of scholarly productivity.

## How Should You Document Archival Research and Special Collections Work?

Archival research forms the empirical foundation of much humanities scholarship. Include a dedicated section listing archives visited, collections consulted, and fellowships received for archival work. For example: "British Library, Manuscript Collections: Consulted the Gladstone Papers (Add MS 44086-44835) and the Macaulay Papers (Add MS 34662-34665), June-August 2024."

Research fellowships that funded archival travel belong in both the grants section and the archival research section. List the fellowship in grants with the funding amount and the archival collections consulted under a separate heading. For scholars whose work depends on rare books, manuscript collections, or special collections, this section provides evidence of research depth that complements the publication record. Some search committees in history and literature departments specifically look for archival research experience when evaluating candidates for positions that require original documentary work.

## How Should You Present Language Skills on a Humanities CV?

Language skills on a humanities CV require both the language and the proficiency level, described in discipline-specific terms. Do not use vague labels like "fluent" or "conversational." Use the proficiency categories recognized by your discipline: reading knowledge, speaking proficiency, or translation ability. For example: "French: reading knowledge (PhD-level translation exam passed, 2023). German: reading knowledge (2 years graduate coursework). Latin: reading knowledge (medieval Latin)."

For scholars in comparative literature, classics, or medieval studies, language skills may be the most important section after publications. Include ancient languages, modern research languages, and any language-based certifications or examinations. For scholars whose research involves translation, note published translations in the publications section and include translation as a separate category under research activities.

## How Should You Describe Your Dissertation on a Humanities CV?

Humanities dissertation descriptions differ from STEM dissertation summaries. Include the full title, committee members, and a 3 to 4 sentence abstract that articulates your argument, methodology, and contribution to the field. For example: "Dissertation: 'The Rhetoric of Reform: Victorian Literature and Social Change, 1832-1884.' Committee: Prof. Sarah Richardson (chair), Prof. Michael Chen, Prof. Elizabeth Torres. This dissertation argues that Victorian reform novels developed a distinctive rhetorical mode that bridged parliamentary debate and popular fiction, reshaping public discourse on labor rights, education reform, and women's property laws. Drawing on archival research in parliamentary records, periodical literature, and 34 novels published between 1832 and 1884, the project identifies 4 rhetorical strategies that novelists adapted from political oratory."

For recent PhDs on the job market, the dissertation abstract serves as a condensed research statement. It communicates your scholarly identity, methodological training, and intellectual contribution in the space that search committees actually read — the first 2 pages of your CV. Place the dissertation description prominently in the education section, even if this makes the section longer than in other disciplines.

## Summary

Humanities academic CVs prioritize monographs, edited volumes, and peer-reviewed articles in that order. Distinguish invited talks from conference presentations with separate sections. Document archival research, special collections consultation, and research fellowships. Present language skills with discipline-specific proficiency categories. Include a substantive dissertation abstract for recent PhDs. Humanities CVs run longer than STEM CVs — 25 to 35 pages for senior scholars — reflecting the narrative detail expected in project descriptions and comprehensive scholarly records.
