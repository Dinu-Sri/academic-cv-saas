Academic CVs that omit required sections leave evaluators with incomplete information. A publications section without a teaching section suggests the candidate neglects pedagogy. A teaching section without a service section suggests the candidate avoids institutional citizenship. Search committees evaluate what is present and what is absent, both carry meaning.

This guide identifies the 7 non-negotiable sections, explains the content each section requires, and specifies which additional sections strengthen applications at different career stages.

## Which 7 Sections Must Every Academic CV Contain?

Every academic CV must contain 7 sections: contact information, education, publications, research experience, teaching experience, grants and funding, and professional service. Omitting any of these sections signals an incomplete application to search committees. (34 words)

These 7 sections are standard across all disciplines, institutions, and career stages. The American Association of University Professors (AAUP), the Chronicle of Higher Education, and university career offices worldwide identify these same 7 sections as required components.

| Section | What It Documents | Why It's Required |
|---------|------------------|------------------|
| Contact information | Identity and accessibility | Reviewers need to reach you and verify your affiliation |
| Education | Formal qualifications | Proves credential requirements are met |
| Publications | Research output | Primary measure of scholarly productivity |
| Research experience | Investigation history | Demonstrates research progression and independence |
| Teaching experience | Pedagogical contribution | Required for all faculty roles |
| Grants and funding | Resource acquisition | Proves ability to sustain a research program |
| Professional service | Community engagement | Demonstrates institutional and disciplinary citizenship |

## What Contact Information Belongs on an Academic CV?

The contact section requires 6 elements: full name, institutional affiliation, department, office address, professional email address, and ORCID identifier. Placing the contact section first enables immediate identification by reviewers. (30 words)

Every piece of contact information serves a verification function:

- **Full name** matches the name on your publications, enabling cross-referencing
- **Institutional affiliation** confirms your current academic position
- **Department** specifies your disciplinary home
- **Office address** establishes your physical presence at the institution
- **Professional email** provides a reliable communication channel (use .edu or institutional domain)
- **ORCID**, a 16-digit persistent identifier that disambiguates researchers with similar names

Optional but increasingly expected:

- Google Scholar profile URL, which enables search committees to verify citation counts
- Personal academic website for expanded information beyond the CV
- LinkedIn profile, less common in academia but accepted in applied fields

Do not include: age, marital status, religious affiliation, or political party membership. These are irrelevant to academic evaluation and illegal to consider in hiring decisions in most jurisdictions.

## What Belongs in the Education Section?

The education section lists every academic degree in reverse chronological order with the degree type, field of study, institution name, graduation date, and dissertation or thesis title for advanced degrees. (31 words)

### Required Elements

For each degree:
- **Degree type and field**, "PhD in Computer Science," "MA in English Literature"
- **Institution**, full name (e.g., "Massachusetts Institute of Technology," not "MIT")
- **Location**, city, state/country
- **Year**, graduation year or "Expected 2026"
- **Dissertation/thesis title**, required for PhD, recommended for MA/MS
- **Advisor**, recommended for PhD

### What Not to Include

- **GPA**, omit after the first academic position; include only for graduate school applications if above 3.5
- **Undergraduate coursework**, omit once you hold a graduate degree
- **High school**, never include

Career stage determines the education section's position in the CV. [The complete guide to writing an academic CV](/blog/how-to-write-academic-cv-complete-guide) explains that PhD students place education first, while faculty place it after publications or research experience.

## What Does a Complete Publications Section Look Like?

A complete publications section lists every scholarly work organized by type, journal articles, book chapters, conference proceedings, using consistent citation formatting with DOI links and the author's name bolded in co-authored works. (33 words)

### Organization Structure

1. **Peer-Reviewed Journal Articles**, the highest-weight category
2. **Books**, authored or edited
3. **Book Chapters**, contributed chapters in edited volumes
4. **Conference Proceedings**, full papers (not abstracts)
5. **Working Papers / Preprints**, arXiv, SSRN, bioRxiv
6. **Under Review**, submitted manuscripts (list journal name or omit)
7. **In Preparation**, use sparingly and only for near-completion manuscripts

### Formatting Rules

- Number entries in reverse chronological order within each category
- **Bold your name** in the author list
- Use the citation style standard for your discipline (APA, MLA, Chicago, IEEE)
- Include DOI as a hyperlink for every published item
- Mark corresponding authorship with an asterisk if relevant in your field

## What Should the Research Experience Section Include?

The research experience section documents every research position held, from undergraduate research assistant to principal investigator, with institution, dates, project title, methods used, and key outcomes. (28 words)

Research experience entries trace the arc of a researcher's development from trainee to independent investigator. Search committees read this section to assess:

- **Technical skill acquisition**, what methods and tools the researcher has mastered
- **Independence progression**, from following protocols to designing studies
- **Collaboration patterns**, whether the researcher has worked across labs, institutions, or disciplines
- **Productivity**, whether research positions produced publications or preliminary data

### Entry Format

```
Postdoctoral Research Fellow                              2020-2023
Department of Neuroscience, Stanford University
Project: Neural circuit dynamics during decision-making in primates
- Designed and executed 3 electrophysiology experiments using 64-channel arrays
- Developed custom Python analysis pipeline for spike sorting and LFP analysis
- Published 2 first-author papers in Nature Neuroscience and Journal of Neuroscience
```

For **PhD students**, include undergraduate research, summer research programs (REU), and lab rotations. For **postdocs**, emphasize independent projects and any preliminary data for future grant applications. For **faculty**, this section often merges with the grants section, since funded research defines the research program.

## What Does a Properly Formatted Teaching Section Look Like?

The teaching section lists all courses taught, TA assignments, guest lectures, and curriculum development contributions in reverse chronological order. Include course titles, enrollment numbers, role (instructor vs. TA), and institution. (31 words)

### Organization Format

Group by institution, then list entries in reverse chronological order:

```
University of Michigan, Department of Chemistry

Instructor of Record
  CHEM 210: Structure and Reactivity I       Fall 2023, Fall 2024 (75 students)
  CHEM 215: Structure and Reactivity II      Winter 2024 (68 students)
  CHEM 453: Biophysical Chemistry (new)      Winter 2025 (32 students)

Teaching Assistant
  CHEM 125: General Chemistry Lab             Fall 2019-Spring 2020 (120 students/semester)
```

### Elements That Strengthen This Section

- **"(new)"** indicator for courses you designed from scratch
- **Student evaluation scores** if above the department mean, e.g., "4.7/5.0 (dept. avg: 4.2)"
- **Teaching awards**, reference them here and in the awards section
- **Pedagogical innovations**, flipped classroom, active learning modules, technology integration

## Why Are the Grants and Service Sections Required?

Grants demonstrate a researcher's ability to secure competitive funding for sustained scholarship. Service demonstrates engagement with the institution and discipline. Tenure committees require evidence of both for promotion. (29 words)

### Grants Section

The grants section proves a researcher can sustain an independent research program. At R1 universities, securing external funding is an explicit tenure requirement. Assistant professors typically need at least one significant external grant (NSF CAREER, NIH R01, or equivalent) before tenure review.

Required elements per grant entry:
- Funding agency and program
- Project title
- Role (PI, Co-PI, Senior Personnel)
- Amount (total and your share)
- Award period

The [guide to listing grants and funding](/blog/grants-funding-academic-cv) covers formatting for federal, foundation, and internal grants.

### Service Section

Service demonstrates institutional citizenship. Junior faculty are expected to serve on 1-2 departmental committees. Senior faculty are expected to chair committees, serve on editorial boards, and hold positions in professional organizations.

Required elements per entry:
- Committee or role name
- Organization (department, university, journal, professional association)
- Dates of service
- Leadership role if applicable (Chair, Vice-Chair)

Service expectations intensify with career stage. Associate professor promotion requires evidence of expanding service beyond the department. Full professor promotion requires national or international disciplinary service.

## Which Additional Sections Should You Consider Adding?

Five supplementary sections, awards, conference presentations, professional development, technical skills, and references, are optional but strengthen specific application types. Add them when the content substantiates claims made in core sections. (33 words)

| Supplementary Section | Add When | Skip When |
|----------------------|----------|-----------|
| Awards and Honors | You have discipline-specific awards, fellowships, or teaching awards | You have only participation certificates |
| Conference Presentations | You have invited talks or 5+ contributed presentations | You have 1-2 poster sessions only |
| Professional Development | You completed relevant certifications or workshops | Items are routine training |
| Technical Skills | Your field values specific tools (programming, lab equipment) | Skills are obvious from your research descriptions |
| References | The application asks for "CV with references" | References are submitted separately |

### How to Decide: Include or Omit?

Apply this test: Does the section provide evidence that evaluators cannot find elsewhere in the CV? Awards substantiate research quality beyond publication counts. Technical skills substantiate methodological breadth beyond project descriptions. Conference presentations substantiate disciplinary visibility beyond publications.

Sections with fewer than 3 entries may weaken rather than strengthen an academic CV. A "Conference Presentations" section with one poster session emphasizes limited conference activity. Combine sparse supplementary content under a broader heading, for example, merge a small presentations list into the service section.
