Academic networking — building professional relationships that advance your scholarly career — translates directly into CV entries. Every invited talk begins with someone who knows your work. Every collaboration begins with a conversation at a conference or a thoughtful email exchange. Every reference letter comes from a scholar who has observed your development over time. Networking is not a separate activity from scholarship. It is the social infrastructure through which scholarly opportunities flow.

The most effective academic networking is interest-based rather than transactional. Reach out to scholars whose work genuinely interests you. Ask thoughtful questions about their research. Share your own work when it connects to theirs. Relationships built on intellectual curiosity endure and produce opportunities over time. Relationships built on "can you give me a job" produce awkward silences and strained conference encounters.

## What Networking Activities Directly Strengthen Your CV?

Five networking activities produce CV entries: attending conferences and presenting your work, participating in workshops and seminars, collaborating on research projects, serving on committees and editorial boards, and engaging with scholars through social media and academic platforms. Each activity requires different time investments and produces different types of CV entries.

Conference attendance produces presentation entries and potential collaborations. Workshop participation produces skills entries and methodological training documentation. Research collaborations produce co-authored publications and grant applications. Committee service produces service entries. Online engagement builds visibility that leads to invitations — to speak, to collaborate, to review. A scholar whose work is known through Twitter or LinkedIn is more likely to receive conference invitations and collaboration requests than one whose work is invisible outside of paywalled journals.

## How Should You Network Effectively at Academic Conferences?

Prepare before attending a conference. Identify 3 to 5 scholars whose work you want to discuss. Read their recent publications. Prepare specific questions about their research. Attend their presentations. Approach them after their talks or during designated networking sessions with your prepared questions. This preparation transforms a generic introduction — "I enjoyed your talk" — into a substantive conversation — "I was interested in your use of instrumental variables to address selection bias. How did you think about the exclusion restriction in this context?"

Follow up after the conference with a brief email referencing your conversation. Attach a paper or resource you discussed. Suggest a future conversation or collaboration if the discussion warranted it. These follow-ups convert brief conference encounters into ongoing professional relationships. Most conference conversations end when the conference ends. The relationships that produce CV entries continue through deliberate follow-up.
