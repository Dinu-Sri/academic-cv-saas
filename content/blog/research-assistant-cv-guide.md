Research assistant positions represent the foundation of many academic careers — they are where you develop laboratory skills, learn research methods, and contribute to projects that become publications. Your research assistant CV must communicate technical competence, methodological training, and the ability to work effectively within a research team. This guide covers how to present research assistant experience for graduate school applications, academic job searches, and lab position transitions.

A research assistant CV differs from a standard academic CV in its emphasis on practical skills and contributions to team projects. While a faculty CV highlights independent research leadership, a research assistant CV highlights collaborative contributions, technical expertise, and the ability to execute protocols reliably. This distinction matters for committees evaluating your readiness for graduate study or your qualification for a more senior research position.

## What Are the 7 Essential Sections of a Research Assistant CV?

A research assistant CV contains 7 essential sections: contact information, education, research experience, technical skills, publications and presentations, relevant coursework, and references. Research experience receives the most detail because it directly demonstrates your qualification for research positions or graduate programs.

The contact section includes your name, institutional email, phone number, and links to relevant profiles. Education lists your degrees in reverse chronological order with GPA if strong. Research experience describes each position with your role, supervisor, dates, specific techniques used, and contributions to project outcomes. Technical skills lists laboratory techniques, instrumentation, software, and analytical methods with proficiency indicators. Publications includes any co-authored papers, even those where your contribution was supporting rather than lead. Presentations lists conference posters or talks you contributed to. Relevant coursework lists advanced classes that demonstrate specialized knowledge. References includes the principal investigators or supervisors who can speak to your research performance.

## How Should You Describe Research Assistant Contributions?

Describe research assistant contributions using the same action verb → task → method → result structure used for independent research, but with verbs that accurately reflect your role: "Performed," "Conducted," "Assisted with," "Supported," "Executed." Do not use leadership verbs like "Designed" or "Led" unless you genuinely designed or led components of the project. Accuracy in role description matters because your references will corroborate your claimed contributions during reference checks.

For example: "Research Assistant, Cancer Biology Lab, supervised by Prof. Maria Chen, 2024-2025. Conducted western blot analysis on 120 protein samples across 4 experimental conditions. Maintained 3 cell lines with weekly passage and contamination testing. Assisted with mouse tumor xenograft experiments, including injection, measurement, and data recording for 45 animals. Analyzed data using GraphPad Prism, contributing to figures 3 and 4 in the resulting publication." This description communicates specific techniques, sample sizes, the scope of responsibility, and the connection to published output — all elements that search committees and graduate admissions panels evaluate.

## How Should You Present Technical Skills Gained as a Research Assistant?

List technical skills with enough specificity that a reader familiar with your field can assess your proficiency. "PCR" is vague — it could mean you observed someone run a PCR once or that you designed primers and optimized thermocycling conditions for 50 experiments. Use qualifiers that indicate depth: "PCR (routine use, 200+ experiments)" or "PCR (basic familiarity)."

Group skills by category for readability: molecular biology techniques, cell culture, animal handling, microscopy, data analysis, programming. Within each category, list the most advanced or frequently used techniques first. For techniques where you have deep expertise, note the volume of experience: "Flow cytometry (300+ samples analyzed, independent operation of BD FACSCanto)." For techniques you learned recently, qualify your level: "CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing (introductory training, 3 constructs generated)." This honest self-assessment helps labs and programs place you at the appropriate level.

## How Do You Address Limited Publication Records as a Research Assistant?

Research assistants often contribute to publications without being listed as authors, or their contributions come late in the publication process when CVs have already been submitted. Address limited publication records with 3 strategies. First, list manuscripts in preparation where your contribution is acknowledged. Second, describe your specific contributions to published papers from your lab, even if you are not an author: "Contributed western blot data to Chen et al. (2024, Cancer Research)." Third, emphasize the skills and techniques sections — programs evaluating research assistants prioritize technical competence over publication metrics.

If your principal investigator will serve as a reference, they can speak to the substance of your contributions during the reference call. Your CV provides the technical evidence. Your reference provides the qualitative assessment. Together they communicate research capability more effectively than either would alone.

## Summary

A research assistant CV emphasizes collaborative contributions, technical skills, and the ability to execute research protocols reliably. Describe contributions with role-appropriate verbs and specific technique descriptions. List technical skills with proficiency qualifiers and experience volumes. Address limited publications through manuscripts in preparation and descriptions of contributions to published work. Use references to complement the technical evidence in your CV with qualitative assessments of your research performance.
