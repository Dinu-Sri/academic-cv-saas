Social sciences occupy a unique position between the quantitative rigor of STEM fields and the interpretive depth of the humanities. Academic CVs in psychology, sociology, economics, and political science must balance publication metrics with narrative research descriptions, quantitative skills with qualitative expertise, and theoretical contributions with policy impact. For comparison, see the [STEM academic CV guide](/blog/stem-academic-cv-science-engineering) and [humanities academic CV guide](/blog/humanities-academic-cv-guide). This guide covers discipline-specific conventions across the major social science fields.

## How Do Social Science CVs Balance Quantitative and Qualitative Research?

Social science CVs present both quantitative and qualitative research using discipline-specific conventions. Psychology and economics emphasize quantitative methods with statistical software proficiency and numerical results. Sociology and political science value both quantitative and qualitative approaches, with dedicated sections for each methodology when the scholar uses both. Anthropology, which bridges social sciences and humanities, may prioritize ethnographic fieldwork and qualitative description over statistical analysis.

The research experience section in social science CVs describes methodology in more detail than STEM CVs because methodological choices are themselves scholarly contributions. For guidance on structuring research descriptions, see [how to write research experience descriptions on your academic CV](/blog/research-experience-academic-cv-description). A psychology research entry includes the study design, sample characteristics, measures used, and analytical approach: "Cross-sectional survey of 850 adolescents. Measured depression symptoms using the CES-D scale. Analyzed data using structural equation modeling in Mplus." A sociology entry might describe both the quantitative and qualitative components of a mixed-methods study: "Conducted 40 semi-structured interviews with first-generation college students and administered surveys to 600 participants across 3 universities. Analyzed interview transcripts using grounded theory and survey data using multilevel regression."

## How Should You Format Publications in Psychology and Related Fields?

Psychology publications follow APA format (7th edition) with authors listed by surname and initials, year in parentheses, article title in sentence case, journal name in italics, volume in italics, and page numbers. For example: "Smith, J. A., Chen, M., & Rodriguez, E. (2024). Effects of mindfulness training on graduate student well-being: A randomized controlled trial. *Journal of Counseling Psychology*, *71*(3), 245-260."

Psychology CVs often separate peer-reviewed articles from other publication types with clear subheadings. Common categories include: "Peer-Reviewed Journal Articles," "Book Chapters," "Conference Presentations," and "Manuscripts Under Review." Some psychology departments expect preprints to be listed separately. Clinical psychology CVs may include a section for treatment manuals, assessment instruments, or clinical training materials. Pre-registered studies and registered reports should be noted as such, as these formats signal methodological rigor valued in psychological science.

## How Should You Present Economics Research and Publications?

Economics CVs follow a distinct format that differs from other social science fields. Publications are typically listed in reverse chronological order without numbering, and working papers receive prominent placement alongside published articles. The job market paper — a near-complete manuscript that represents your best work — appears at the top of the research section for PhD candidates on the job market, often labeled as "Job Market Paper" with a brief abstract.

Economics values working papers and policy briefs as evidence of an active research pipeline. A typical economics CV includes sections for "Publications," "Working Papers," "Work in Progress," and "Policy Papers" or "Policy Briefs." The publications section may be relatively short for junior scholars, but the working papers section should demonstrate productivity and a clear research trajectory. For each working paper, include co-author names, a brief abstract, and the current status: "Submitted to *American Economic Review*," "Revise and resubmit at *Quarterly Journal of Economics*," or "Available as NBER Working Paper No. 31245."

## How Should You Document Policy Impact and Public Engagement?

Policy impact matters in political science, public policy, and applied economics. Include a dedicated section for policy contributions, expert testimony, commissioned reports, or media appearances that demonstrate the real-world relevance of your research. For example: "Expert testimony before the U.S. Senate Committee on Health, Education, Labor, and Pensions, Hearing on Mental Health Services in Higher Education, March 2025." Or: "Policy brief on affordable housing strategies adopted by the City of Seattle Office of Housing, resulting in 3 recommendations incorporated into the 2025 Housing Action Plan."

Media engagement also belongs in this section: "Cited in *The New York Times*, 'Why College Mental Health Services Are Overwhelmed,' October 2024." Some social science departments explicitly value public scholarship and policy engagement in tenure and promotion criteria. Check your target department's guidelines and present policy impact in a format that matches their expectations.

## How Should You Present Statistical and Methodological Skills?

Social science CVs list statistical and methodological skills with specificity that signals readiness for independent research. Group skills by category: statistical methods, software proficiency, research designs, and specialized techniques. For example: "Statistical Methods: Multilevel modeling, structural equation modeling, longitudinal data analysis, survival analysis, meta-analysis. Software: R (advanced), Stata (advanced), Mplus (intermediate), Python (intermediate). Research Designs: Randomized controlled trials, quasi-experimental designs, survey methodology, qualitative comparative analysis."

For mixed-methods researchers, include both quantitative and qualitative skills: "Qualitative Methods: Semi-structured interviewing, focus group facilitation, grounded theory, thematic analysis, ethnographic observation. Software: NVivo, Dedoose." The skills section in social science CVs often runs longer than in other fields because methodological training is a primary selection criterion for both academic positions and postdoctoral fellowships.

## How Do You Present Teaching Across Different Types of Courses?

Social science teaching often spans multiple course types: large introductory lectures, methods courses, substantive seminars, and graduate-level theory courses. Present teaching experience organized by course type rather than chronologically to help search committees assess your versatility. Include enrollment numbers for large courses and student evaluation scores when available.

Teaching descriptions in social science CVs should mention the pedagogical approach and any teaching innovations. For example: "Developed and taught 'Introduction to Research Methods' (PSYC 301) — a required course for psychology majors covering experimental design, survey methodology, and statistical analysis. Redesigned the curriculum around hands-on data analysis projects using R, replacing traditional textbook exercises. Average enrollment: 65 students per semester. Average evaluation: 4.5/5.0." This description communicates course scope, pedagogical innovation, enrollment scale, and teaching effectiveness in a compact format.

Social science academic CVs balance quantitative and qualitative research presentation with discipline-specific conventions. Psychology uses APA format with methodological detail.
