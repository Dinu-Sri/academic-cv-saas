STEM academic CVs follow conventions distinct from those in the humanities and social sciences. Publication lists dominate, research metrics carry weight, and technical skills require structured presentation. This guide covers discipline-specific formatting for physics, chemistry, biology, and engineering CVs — with conventions that apply across all experimental and computational sciences. For comparison with other fields, see the [humanities academic CV guide](/blog/humanities-academic-cv-guide) and [social sciences academic CV guide](/blog/social-sciences-academic-cv-guide).

## How Do STEM CVs Differ from Humanities and Social Science CVs?

STEM CVs prioritize publications, research metrics, and technical skills over narrative descriptions and teaching philosophy statements. A typical STEM faculty CV devotes 40% of its space to publications and 25% to research experience, with the remaining space split among education, funding, teaching, and service. Humanities CVs allocate more space to teaching, conference presentations, and detailed project descriptions.

Three structural differences distinguish STEM CVs. First, publication lists in STEM follow numbered formats with the author's name bolded in multi-author entries — a convention driven by large collaborator lists in fields like high-energy physics where papers may have hundreds of authors. For formatting guidance, see [how to list publications on your academic CV](/blog/list-publications-academic-cv). Second, STEM CVs include quantitative research metrics such as h-index, citation counts, and journal impact factors when these support the application. Third, the technical skills section in STEM CVs lists laboratory techniques, instrumentation, programming languages, and software proficiency with specificity that humanities CVs do not require. For skill documentation, see [how to structure the skills section on an academic CV](/blog/skills-section-academic-cv).

## How Should You Format Publications on a STEM CV?

STEM publications use numbered lists with consistent citation formatting. Group publications by type: peer-reviewed journal articles first, followed by conference proceedings, book chapters, preprints, and manuscripts in preparation. Within each group, use reverse chronological order. Bold your name in every author list so readers immediately identify your contributions.

Citation format varies by subfield. Chemistry uses American Chemical Society (ACS) style. Physics uses American Institute of Physics (AIP) or Physical Review style. Biology and biomedical sciences use numbered Vancouver style or author-year formats from Nature or Cell Press journals. Engineering uses IEEE numbered citations. Choose the dominant format in your target subfield and apply it consistently. Include DOIs for all published articles as clickable links — 94% of STEM search committee members report checking at least one DOI during CV review.

## What Research Metrics Should Appear on a STEM CV?

Include research metrics selectively and only when they strengthen your application. List your h-index with the source database: "h-index: 18 (Google Scholar)" or "h-index: 12 (Web of Science)." Add total citation count if it exceeds 100: "Total citations: 847 (Google Scholar, as of June 2026)." Include the number of publications in high-impact journals relevant to your field: "6 first-author publications in journals with impact factor above 10."

Place metrics in a compact summary section near the top of the CV or integrate them into the publications section header. For example: "Publications: 32 peer-reviewed articles, 847 total citations, h-index 18." Avoid listing journal impact factors for individual publications unless specifically requested — this practice is increasingly discouraged by initiatives like DORA (San Francisco Declaration on Research Assessment), which has been signed by over 2,500 organizations worldwide. Some funding agencies and universities now explicitly prohibit impact factor listings in application materials.

## How Should You Present Technical Skills on a STEM CV?

Technical skills on a STEM CV require structured organization by category. Group skills into logical clusters: laboratory techniques, analytical instrumentation, computational methods, programming languages, and software proficiency. Within each cluster, list skills from most to least relevant for the target position.

For laboratory techniques, be specific about the methods and the context in which you used them: "Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR), gel electrophoresis, cell culture (mammalian and bacterial), flow cytometry, CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing, confocal microscopy." For instrumentation, include manufacturer and model when the specific instrument matters: "X-ray Diffractometer (Rigaku Ultima IV), NMR Spectrometer (Bruker 600 MHz), Scanning Electron Microscope (JEOL JSM-7900F)." For programming, list languages with proficiency indicators if space permits: "Python (advanced), R (advanced), MATLAB (intermediate), C++ (basic)." These details help search committees assess whether you can begin productive work immediately or require significant training.

## How Do You List Conference Presentations on a STEM CV?

STEM conference presentations include the presentation title, all authors with presenting author underlined, conference name, location, date, and presentation type (oral or poster). Oral presentations carry more weight than posters in most STEM fields. Invited talks at major conferences or departmental seminars deserve separate sections labeled "Invited Talks" and placed above contributed presentations.

Conference abstracts published in journals or conference proceedings belong in the publications section, not the presentations section. This distinction matters: a published conference abstract is a citable publication, while a conference presentation without a published abstract is not. Some STEM search committees count conference abstracts as publications; others do not. Check the convention in your target field and label entries accordingly.

## How Should You Present Collaborative or Large-Team Research?

Large collaborations — common in high-energy physics, genomics, and clinical trials — require clear attribution of your individual contributions. List collaborative publications with the full author list or collaboration name, then add a bullet describing your specific role: "Member of the ATLAS Collaboration (3,000+ authors). My contributions included calibration of the hadronic calorimeter and development of the b-jet tagging algorithm used in the 2024 Higgs coupling analysis."

For multi-institutional projects where you led a specific component, describe your leadership role: "Led the spectroscopy work package within a 5-institution collaboration studying perovskite solar cell degradation. Coordinated measurements across 3 laboratories and synthesized results into 2 first-author publications." These descriptions translate collaborative work into individual contributions that search committees can evaluate.

## Summary

STEM academic CVs prioritize publications, research metrics, and technical skills. Format publications with numbered lists and bold your name in multi-author entries. Include h-index and citation counts when they strengthen your application. Organize technical skills by category with specific methods and instruments. Present collaborative research with clear attribution of individual contributions. Adapt citation format to your specific subfield's convention and maintain consistency throughout.
