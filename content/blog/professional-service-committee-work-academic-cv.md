Professional service documents your contributions to the academic community beyond research and teaching. Search committees evaluate service as evidence of academic citizenship — your willingness to contribute to departmental governance, peer review, conference organization, and professional society leadership. Service carries different weight depending on the institution type and career stage. Research universities value service that demonstrates disciplinary leadership. Teaching colleges value service that supports institutional operations and student development.

Service falls into 3 categories: departmental service (committees, program coordination, faculty governance), disciplinary service (journal reviewing, conference organizing, professional society roles), and community service (outreach, public engagement, consulting). Each category provides different types of evidence about your scholarly citizenship. Departmental service shows you contribute to institutional operations. Disciplinary service shows you contribute to your field's infrastructure. Community service shows you connect scholarly expertise to broader social needs.

## What Types of Service Should Appear on an Academic CV?

Include service activities that demonstrate meaningful contribution rather than token participation. Serving on a departmental curriculum committee for 2 years with documented contributions — revising program requirements, developing new courses, assessing learning outcomes — represents substantive service. Attending 2 committee meetings without contributing represents token participation. Search committees can distinguish between these levels of engagement through the detail you provide.

Journal reviewing forms a significant component of disciplinary service. List journals you have reviewed for, with the number of reviews when substantial: "Ad hoc reviewer for Journal of Applied Physics (8 manuscripts), Physical Review B (5 manuscripts), and Nano Letters (3 manuscripts)." Editorships and editorial board memberships carry more weight than ad hoc reviewing and should appear prominently. Conference organizing — serving on program committees, organizing symposia, chairing sessions — demonstrates disciplinary leadership. Professional society roles — elected positions, committee chairs, working group membership — signal recognition by your peers beyond your home institution.

## How Should You Format the Service Section?

Organize service into clearly labeled subsections: "Departmental Service," "Disciplinary Service," "Community and Professional Service." Within each subsection, list entries in reverse chronological order. Include your role, the committee or organization name, and dates. For substantive roles, add a brief description of responsibilities and accomplishments: "Graduate Admissions Committee (2023-2025). Reviewed 180 applications annually. Revised evaluation rubric, reducing committee deliberation time by 30% while maintaining inter-rater reliability above 0.85."

For reviewing and editorial work, use a compact format that communicates volume without consuming excessive space: "Grant Reviewer: National Science Foundation (4 panels), Netherlands Organisation for Scientific Research (2 panels)." This format conveys the activity and its frequency efficiently. For conference organizing, include the conference name, your role, and the year: "Program Committee Member, International Conference on Machine Learning (ICML), 2025."

## How Does Service Weight Differ Across Institution Types?

Research universities value disciplinary service — journal editing, conference organizing, grant reviewing, professional society leadership — because these activities enhance the institution's scholarly reputation and your professional network. Departmental service matters at research universities primarily at the tenure review stage. Before tenure, research universities expect faculty to protect their research time by limiting departmental service to essential committees.

Teaching colleges value departmental service highly because institutional operations depend on faculty governance contributions. Curriculum development, assessment coordination, advising program management, and faculty search committees count significantly toward promotion at teaching-focused institutions. Community service — partnerships with local schools, public lectures, consulting for community organizations — also carries more weight at teaching colleges than at research universities because these institutions emphasize community engagement as part of their educational mission.

## How Much Service Is Enough for Tenure Review?

Service expectations for tenure vary by institution type. Research universities typically expect service contributions that demonstrate disciplinary citizenship without compromising research productivity. A typical tenure dossier at a research university includes membership on 2 to 3 departmental committees, ad hoc reviewing for 3 to 5 journals, and participation in 1 professional society committee or conference organizing role. Total service should represent approximately 10 to 15% of your professional effort.

Teaching colleges expect more substantial service records. A tenure dossier at a liberal arts college may include chairing 1 to 2 departmental committees, serving on 2 to 3 college-wide committees, advising student organizations, participating in community partnerships, and contributing to professional societies. Service may represent 20 to 30% of professional effort. Document service contributions with the same specificity you apply to research: describe the problem addressed, your specific contribution, and the outcome achieved.
