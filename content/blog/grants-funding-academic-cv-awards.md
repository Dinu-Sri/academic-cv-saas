Grants and funding entries on an academic CV demonstrate that external reviewers have validated your research ideas and awarded resources to support them. Search committees read the funding section as evidence of research competitiveness, proposal writing skill, and your ability to sustain a research program. A well-organized funding section distinguishes candidates who can attract resources from those who have not yet demonstrated this capability.

The grants section occupies a prominent position on faculty and postdoctoral CVs — typically immediately after publications or research experience. Early-career researchers may place it after education if their funding record is still developing. The section's placement signals its importance: the closer to the top, the more weight search committees assign to grant acquisition as a selection criterion. For guidance on positioning funding in different application contexts, see [how to tailor your academic CV for different positions](/blog/tailor-academic-cv-different-positions).

## What Information Should Each Grant Entry Include?

Each grant entry requires 6 elements: funding agency or organization, program or grant name, project title, dollar amount, your role, and dates. This structure provides committees with the information they need to assess funding competitiveness, project scope, and your level of responsibility.

The funding agency name signals prestige: "National Science Foundation (NSF)" communicates a different level of competitiveness than "University Internal Research Grant." The program name adds specificity: "NSF Division of Materials Research, CAREER Award." The project title should match the official grant title as awarded. The dollar amount uses the total award unless you held a sub-award from a larger grant, in which case note both: "$45,000 (sub-award from NIH R01 HL123456, $2.1M total)." Your role distinguishes PI, Co-PI, Co-Investigator, or project contributor. Dates include the award period start and end: "2024-2027."

## How Should You Prioritize and Categorize Grant Entries?

Organize grants into categories by type and prestige. External grants — those awarded by national funding agencies, foundations, or international organizations — appear first. Internal grants — university seed funding, departmental travel awards, or small institutional support — appear second. Fellowships occupy their own category, placed between external and internal grants or in a separate section entirely if you hold multiple prestigious fellowships. The [postdoc CV guide](/blog/postdoc-cv-research-independence) covers how to present grant applications and fellowships during the critical transition to research independence.

Within each category, list grants in reverse chronological order. For similarly prestigious grants, the dollar amount determines order: larger awards first. A $500,000 NSF grant appears before a $25,000 foundation grant even if the foundation grant is more recent. For early-career researchers, include graduate fellowships and small awards that demonstrate a trajectory of successful grant-seeking. The NSF Graduate Research Fellowship, Fulbright grants, and similar competitive awards carry weight throughout your career.

## How Do You Handle Pending and Unfunded Grant Applications?

Pending grant applications belong on your CV with the status clearly indicated: "Under review at National Institutes of Health (NIH), R01 mechanism, $1.2M requested." These entries demonstrate an active funding pipeline and proposal writing activity. Search committees at research universities expect to see evidence that you are continuously pursuing external funding.

Unfunded applications require judgment. If you applied for a competitive grant and received reviewer feedback, you may list it as "Not funded" with the submission date and agency to demonstrate effort. However, list unfunded grants sparingly — 1 or 2 entries at most. If you have a pattern of unfunded applications without awards to balance them, omit the unfunded entries entirely. They signal effort but also signal that reviewers have not found your proposals competitive, which may raise questions rather than demonstrate capability.

## How Should You Present Fellowships on an Academic CV?

Fellowships differ from grants in that they typically fund individuals rather than projects. List fellowships in a separate section from research grants, using the same 6-element format: funding organization, fellowship name, your proposed research area or project title, stipend or award amount, your role (fellow or awardee), and dates. For example: "National Science Foundation, Graduate Research Fellowship, 'Machine Learning Approaches to Protein Structure Prediction,' $138,000 (3 years), 2022-2025."

Prestigious fellowships — Rhodes, Marshall, Fulbright, NSF GRFP, NIH F31/F32, Mellon, ACLS — deserve prominent placement. These awards signal that you have been identified as exceptional by national-level selection committees. List them even decades into your career. A senior professor's CV should still include an NSF Graduate Research Fellowship from 1998 because the award's prestige persists.

## How Should You List Smaller Awards and Honors?

Smaller awards — best paper prizes, travel grants, departmental teaching awards — belong in a combined "Awards and Honors" section separate from major grants and fellowships. This section uses a simpler format: award name, awarding organization, and year. For example: "Best Paper Award, American Sociological Association, Section on Mathematical Sociology, 2025." Omit dollar amounts for awards under $5,000 unless the amount is notable in context.

Separate competitive awards from recognition awards. Competitive awards — those you applied for and were selected from an applicant pool — carry more weight. Recognition awards — those conferred without application, such as being named to a university honor roll — provide context about your standing but communicate less about your ability to compete for resources. If space is limited, prioritize competitive awards and omit recognition-based honors.

## How Does the Grants Section Change Across Career Stages?

The grants section grows and shifts emphasis as your career advances. Doctoral students and postdocs list fellowships prominently because they likely have few research grants. Assistant professors emphasize their first external grants — the first NIH R01 or NSF CAREER award represents a major career milestone. Associate and full professors with extensive funding histories may select only major grants from the past 5 to 10 years, omitting smaller internal awards to keep the section focused.

Senior researchers with 20 or more major grants may organize the section by funding agency rather than chronologically: "National Science Foundation Grants," "National Institutes of Health Grants," "Foundation Grants." This organization helps committees quickly assess funding breadth across agencies. Always include total career funding at the top of the section when the amount is substantial: "Total external research funding: $4.2M (as PI), $8.7M (as Co-I)."

## Summary

The grants section of an academic CV demonstrates research competitiveness and the ability to attract resources. Include funding agency, program name, project title, dollar amount, your role, and dates for each entry. Organize by category: external grants, fellowships, internal grants, and awards. List pending applications to show an active pipeline. Separate competitive awards from recognition honors. Adjust scope across career stages — from comprehensive early-career listings to selective senior summaries organized by funding agency.
