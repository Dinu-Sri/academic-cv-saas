Search committees reviewing 100 applications for a single position spend an average of 30 to 60 seconds on an initial CV scan. In that time, formatting errors, missing information, and vague language can move your application from the "consider" pile to the "reject" pile before the committee reads a single publication title. Avoiding common mistakes ensures that your CV survives the initial screening and reaches the detailed review stage.

Academic CV mistakes fall into 3 categories: formatting errors that signal carelessness, content gaps that weaken your scholarly profile, and language problems that obscure your accomplishments. Each category contains identifiable patterns that you can systematically check and correct. This guide complements the [complete academic CV writing guide](/blog/how-to-write-academic-cv-complete-guide) by focusing on what NOT to do.

## Mistake 1: Inconsistent Formatting Across Sections

Inconsistent formatting is the most common academic CV mistake and the easiest for search committees to spot. Mixed citation styles in the publications section, varying date formats across entries, and inconsistent font sizes or spacing patterns signal a lack of attention to detail that committees extrapolate to research quality.

Fix this by choosing one citation format — APA, MLA, Chicago, or your discipline's standard — and applying it to every publication entry. For a complete treatment of citation conventions, see [how to list publications on your academic CV](/blog/list-publications-academic-cv). Use the same date format throughout: "September 2024" or "09/2024" but never both. Maintain identical spacing between section headings and body text. Use the same font size and style for all body text and all headings. After making changes, print your CV and review it on paper — formatting inconsistencies that escape detection on screen become visible in print.

## Mistake 2: Missing or Incomplete Contact Information

An academic CV without a current institutional email address, ORCID identifier, and professional website link appears incomplete to search committees. These elements signal that you are an active member of the scholarly community with a verifiable research identity.

Include your full name as it appears in publications, your current institutional affiliation, a professional email address, and your ORCID identifier. Add a link to your Google Scholar profile or personal academic website if you maintain one. For a complete checklist, see [how to set up your online academic profiles — ORCID and Google Scholar](/blog/online-academic-profiles-orcid-google-scholar). Omit personal details that could introduce bias: age, marital status, number of children, and photographs are not included on academic CVs in most countries and may create legal exposure for search committees.

## Mistake 3: Vague Research Descriptions Without Quantified Outcomes

Describing research with phrases like "worked on" or "was involved in" without specifying methods, scope, or results fails to communicate your actual contribution. Search committees evaluate research experience descriptions to assess whether you can design studies, execute methods, and produce meaningful findings independently.

Fix vague descriptions by following the action verb → task → method → result structure. Replace "Worked on a project about climate change and agriculture" with "Analyzed 30 years of satellite-derived vegetation indices for 12 crop types across the Midwestern United States. Identified a 14% decline in growing season length correlated with rising nighttime temperatures." The first description could describe any level of involvement. The second communicates data analysis skill, geographic and temporal scope, and a specific finding.

## Mistake 4: Omitting Works in Progress and Research Pipeline

Excluding manuscripts under review, in preparation, or in revision creates unnecessary gaps in your publication record. Search committees understand that academic publishing timelines span months to years and evaluate candidates based on research trajectory, not just published output at a single point in time.

Include a "Works in Progress" subsection within your publications section. List manuscripts under review with the journal name and submission date. List manuscripts in preparation with a working title and target journal. These entries demonstrate active scholarship and help committees project your publication record 1 to 2 years into the future — exactly the timeframe relevant to hiring and tenure decisions.

## Mistake 5: Padding with Irrelevant Information

Undergraduate club memberships, high school achievements, and unrelated work experience dilute the professional focus of an academic CV. These entries consume space that should showcase research, teaching, and service — the categories search committees actually evaluate.

Remove any entry that does not directly support your candidacy for an academic position. This includes high school information unless you are an undergraduate student, part-time jobs unrelated to your field unless they demonstrate transferable skills, and hobbies unless they connect to your scholarly identity. Every line on your CV competes for the reader's limited attention. Make each line earn its place.

## Mistake 6: Using Passive Voice to Describe Your Contributions

Passive voice obscures who did what in research descriptions. "A survey was conducted" does not tell the reader whether you designed the survey, distributed it, analyzed the results, or simply attended a meeting where someone mentioned a survey being conducted. Academic CVs require active voice with clear attribution of individual contributions.

Replace passive constructions with active verbs that specify your role: "Designed and administered a survey of 450 participants" or "Conducted semi-structured interviews with 30 healthcare administrators." If you contributed to a larger team effort, specify your component: "Led the statistical analysis work package for a 5-institution clinical trial." Active voice communicates ownership and capability. Passive voice communicates ambiguity.

## Mistake 7: Neglecting to Bold Your Name in Multi-Author Publications

In publications with 3 or more authors, search committees scan author lists looking for the candidate's name. If your name blends into the list unhighlighted, the committee may miss your contributions entirely or underestimate your authorship position. Bolding your name solves this problem instantly and is standard practice across all academic disciplines.

Apply bold formatting to your name in every publication entry. For large collaborations, add a parenthetical note specifying your contribution: "(Member of the LIGO Scientific Collaboration. Contributed to detector characterization and data quality analysis.)" This note transforms a collaborative publication entry from a name on a long list into a documented individual contribution.

## Mistake 8: Using Jargon Without Explanation

Discipline-specific jargon that your target audience understands strengthens your CV by demonstrating expertise. Jargon from a different subfield that your audience may not recognize creates confusion and may signal poor communication skills. This is particularly important for interdisciplinary positions where committee members come from multiple disciplines.

Define specialized terms when applying outside your immediate subfield. A condensed matter physicist applying to a materials science department should explain techniques like "angle-resolved photoemission spectroscopy (ARPES)" on first use. A digital humanities scholar applying to an English department should briefly define computational methods unfamiliar to literary scholars. Err on the side of brief explanation. One parenthetical sentence can prevent a committee member from dismissing your expertise as incomprehensible.

## Mistake 9: Using an Unprofessional Email Address

Your email address appears at the top of your CV — the first thing many search committee members read. An unprofessional address like "partyanimal@email.com" or "cuteresearcher123@university.edu" undermines the professional impression you work to establish throughout the rest of the document. Use your institutional email address. If you do not have one, create a professional address using your name: "jane.smith@email.com."

Avoid email addresses that will expire or change. Your institutional email typically deactivates when you leave the institution. If you are on the job market and may lose access to your current institutional email, create a permanent professional address and use it alongside your institutional address during the transition period.

## Mistake 10: Failing to Proofread After Every Update

Academic CVs accumulate errors through incremental updates. You add a publication, adjust the formatting, and accidentally misalign the date column for the entry below it. You change your advisor's title and introduce a typo. You add a grant and forget to update the total funding amount at the top of the section. These small errors compound over time and become visible to committees scanning for any reason to reduce an applicant pool.

Proofread your CV after every update. Read it aloud to catch sentence-level errors. Ask a colleague to review it — fresh eyes catch mistakes you have become blind to. Print the final version and review it on paper before submitting. These steps add 15 minutes to your application process and prevent errors that could cost you an interview.
