The same academic CV submitted to every position signals a lack of effort and strategic thinking. Search committees at different institution types evaluate candidates using different criteria. A research university committee prioritizes publications and grants. A teaching college committee looks for pedagogical training and student outcomes first. An industry hiring manager scans for transferable technical skills and project management experience. Tailoring your CV for each type of position increases your chances of advancing past the initial screening round.

Tailoring involves 3 distinct operations: reordering sections to match institutional priorities, adjusting the level of detail for different types of entries, and selectively including or omitting entries based on relevance. These operations require a master CV that contains your complete academic record, from which you derive tailored versions for specific applications. The [complete guide to writing an academic CV](/blog/how-to-write-academic-cv-complete-guide) covers building and maintaining this master document.

## How Should You Tailor a CV for Research-Intensive University Positions?

Research university CVs lead with research experience and publications. Place these sections on the first page. Include detailed descriptions of research projects with methodologies and outcomes. List all publications, including manuscripts under review and works in progress. For formatting guidance, see [how to list publications on an academic CV](/blog/list-publications-academic-cv). Add research metrics — h-index, citation counts, journal impact factors where appropriate — in a compact summary near the top. The [grants and funding section](/blog/grants-funding-academic-cv-awards) should appear prominently, as research universities weigh external funding as a primary evaluation criterion.

The teaching section for research university CVs should be concise: 1 to 2 lines per course covering the course name, your role, enrollment, and semester. Include quantitative evaluation scores if available. Research universities want evidence that you can teach competently without compromising research productivity. A long, detailed teaching section on a research university CV signals misplaced priorities. For guidance on structuring teaching entries, see [how to document teaching experience on an academic CV](/blog/teaching-experience-academic-cv-guide). Place the teaching section after research and publications, not before them. Service entries should be minimal — departmental committee membership, a line or two of professional service — unless you are applying for a position that specifically values service contributions such as department chair or program director roles.

## How Should You Tailor a CV for Teaching-Focused Colleges?

Teaching college CVs lead with teaching experience and pedagogical training. Place these sections on the first page, above research experience. Expand teaching descriptions to 3 to 4 lines per course covering course design, teaching methods, assessment strategies, and student outcomes. Include a separate section for pedagogical training: workshops attended, certificates earned, teaching observation programs completed.

The research section for teaching college CVs should emphasize work that involves undergraduates and projects that can continue with limited resources. A liberal arts college committee wants to know that you can maintain an active research program that includes undergraduate collaborators, not that you require a $500,000 laboratory startup. Describe research projects with an emphasis on undergraduate mentorship: "Supervised 4 undergraduate researchers who presented at national conferences and co-authored 2 publications." Include a statement of teaching philosophy or a link to a teaching portfolio in your contact section or as a separate entry.

## How Should You Tailor a CV for Postdoctoral Positions?

Postdoc CVs emphasize research potential and alignment with the prospective lab's research program. Place research experience and publications on the first page. Include a 3 to 4 sentence research statement at the top that articulates your current research interests and how they connect to the position. Postdoc selection committees read this statement carefully because it signals whether you have thought about how your expertise complements the lab's existing strengths. For a complete treatment, see [building a CV for postdoctoral positions](/blog/postdoc-cv-research-independence).

Tailor your technical skills section to match the specific techniques and methods used in the target lab. If the lab uses CRISPR-based gene editing, list your relevant molecular biology experience prominently. If the lab does computational modeling, list programming languages and software packages before laboratory techniques. Postdoc advisors hire based on specific technical fit and evidence of research productivity, not general academic excellence. Your CV should make the case that you can contribute to ongoing projects from day one while developing your independent research direction.

## How Should You Tailor a CV for Industry and Non-Academic Positions?

Industry CVs use a fundamentally different structure from academic CVs. Replace the publications section with a "Selected Publications" list of 3 to 5 most relevant papers. Add a professional summary at the top — 3 to 4 sentences describing your expertise, technical skills, and the value you bring to organizations. Translate academic accomplishments into business language: "Published 12 peer-reviewed articles" becomes "Communicated complex technical findings to diverse audiences through publications, presentations, and reports." For a complete guide, see [transitioning from academia to industry — CV adaptation strategies](/blog/transitioning-academia-industry-cv-adaptation).

Add sections that do not appear on academic CVs: project management experience, industry collaborations, patents, and leadership roles. Describe research projects in terms of problem solved, methods used, and business impact: "Developed a machine learning model that reduced quality control inspection time by 40%, saving $200,000 annually in manufacturing costs." Academic CVs emphasize scholarly contribution; industry CVs emphasize measurable business outcomes. Remove sections irrelevant to the target role: teaching experience, academic service, and conference presentations unless they demonstrate transferable skills like communication or project coordination.

## How Do You Decide What to Remove When Tailoring?

Every entry on a tailored CV must answer the question: "Does this help this specific committee evaluate me for this specific position?" If the answer is no, remove the entry. Undergraduate club memberships, high school achievements, and irrelevant work experience dilute the professional focus of your CV. A 10-page CV with 10 highly relevant pages outperforms a 15-page CV with 5 pages of filler.

The removal decision depends on career stage. PhD students and postdocs should keep most entries because their record is still developing. Mid-career faculty can remove older, less relevant entries — the undergraduate poster presentation from 2008 no longer matters for a tenure review in 2026. The [assistant professor CV guide](/blog/assistant-professor-cv-tenure-track) explains which entries carry weight at different pre-tenure stages. Senior faculty should curate aggressively: select the 15 most significant publications rather than listing all 80. Search committees reviewing senior candidates look for sustained excellence and impact, not comprehensive documentation of early-career activities.

## How Many Versions of Your CV Should You Maintain?

Maintain 1 master CV containing your complete academic record — every publication, presentation, course, grant, and service activity. Create 3 to 4 tailored versions for different types of positions: research university, teaching college, postdoc or fellowship, and industry. Update the master CV continuously as new accomplishments occur. Generate tailored versions from the master only when preparing a specific application.
