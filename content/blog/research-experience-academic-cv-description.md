Research experience entries form the core of an academic CV. Search committees, fellowship reviewers, and hiring managers read this section first to assess your scholarly trajectory and research capabilities. Strong research descriptions use specific methodologies, quantified outcomes, and clear attribution of your individual contributions. Weak descriptions rely on vague language and passive voice that obscures what you actually did.

## What Is the Standard Structure for a Research Experience Entry?

Each research experience entry contains 5 components: project title, institution and dates, your role, a 3 to 5 bullet description of responsibilities and outcomes, and associated publications. This structure provides committees with the who, what, when, where, and how of your research contributions in a scannable format.

The project title names the research area or specific project. The institution line includes the university or organization name, department or lab, and location. Dates use the start and end months and years: "September 2023 – August 2025." Your role uses a specific title: "Postdoctoral Researcher," "Graduate Research Assistant," "Principal Investigator." The bullet description follows a consistent format: action verb, specific task, method used, and quantified outcome. Associated publications link the project to verifiable scholarly output.

## How Do You Write Effective Research Experience Bullet Points?

Start each bullet with a strong action verb that signals your level of contribution. Use "Designed," "Developed," "Led," or "Conceptualized" for projects you initiated. Use "Executed," "Implemented," "Conducted," or "Performed" for work you carried out under supervision. The verb choice communicates ownership and independence to committees scanning for research leadership potential.

Follow the action verb with the specific task, the method or technique used, and a quantified outcome. For example: "Designed a randomized controlled trial with 340 participants across 4 sites to evaluate a cognitive behavioral intervention for anxiety disorders. Measured treatment effects using linear mixed models, identifying a 28% reduction in symptom severity in the treatment group compared to controls." This bullet communicates study design, sample size, analytical method, and result magnitude in 2 sentences. Compare this to a weak alternative: "Worked on a study about anxiety treatment." The weak version uses passive voice, omits methods, and provides no measurable outcome.

## How Many Bullet Points Should Each Research Entry Have?

Each research experience entry needs 3 to 5 bullet points to fully describe a project. One or 2 bullets cannot capture the complexity of a research project. More than 6 bullets overwhelm readers and suggest an inability to prioritize information. The sweet spot of 3 to 5 bullets allows you to describe the project's objective, your role, the methodology, key findings, and any broader impacts or publications.

For long-term projects spanning multiple years, focus bullets on the most significant outcomes and your highest-impact contributions. A 5-year doctoral project may generate 10 publications, but your CV entry needs only the 4 most influential papers. For short-term projects like summer research placements, 2 to 3 bullets suffice to describe the experience without overrepresenting a brief engagement.

## How Do You Describe Collaborative Research Without Overclaiming?

Collaborative research requires careful language that acknowledges teamwork while clarifying your specific contribution. Use phrases like "Co-developed with a team of 4 researchers" or "Led the computational modeling component of a 12-person collaboration." These constructions give credit to collaborators while specifying your role within the larger project.

Avoid the first-person singular for genuinely collaborative work: "I discovered" misrepresents team science. Instead, use "Contributed to the discovery of" or "Participated in the identification of" for work where you played a meaningful but not lead role. For multi-author publications that result from collaborative projects, the publication entry itself serves as documentation of your contribution — the research description need not repeat author order details.

## How Should You Present Negative or Inconclusive Research Results?

Negative results and inconclusive findings belong on your CV when they represent substantial research effort. Describe them as you would positive results, focusing on the methodological rigor and scientific contribution rather than the outcome direction. For example: "Tested the hypothesis that compound X inhibits tumor growth in a murine model. Across 3 independent experiments with 15 animals per group, observed no statistically significant difference between treatment and control groups (p = 0.42). Results published as a registered report in Journal of Negative Results."

Reporting negative results demonstrates scientific integrity and methodological competence. It also shows that you complete projects and publish regardless of outcome — a trait valued by search committees who have seen too many CVs with mysterious gaps where failed experiments were simply omitted. Include negative result publications in your publication list with the same formatting as positive result papers.

## How Does Research Experience Formatting Differ Across Disciplines?

STEM fields prioritize methods and quantitative outcomes. A chemistry research bullet reads: "Synthesized 12 novel organometallic complexes and characterized structures using X-ray crystallography and NMR spectroscopy. Measured catalytic activity for CO2 reduction, identifying 3 compounds with turnover frequencies exceeding 500 h⁻¹." Social sciences emphasize study design, sample characteristics, and analytical approaches: "Conducted semi-structured interviews with 45 healthcare workers across 6 hospitals. Analyzed transcripts using grounded theory methodology, identifying 4 primary themes related to burnout and institutional support." Humanities research focuses on archival work, theoretical frameworks, and interpretive contributions: "Examined 18th-century parish records from 3 dioceses in northern England. Applied postcolonial theoretical frameworks to reconstruct patterns of religious practice among marginalized communities."

Each discipline values different types of evidence. STEM committees look for technical proficiency and productivity metrics. Social science committees value methodological rigor and sample representativeness. Humanities committees assess interpretive sophistication and archival depth. Tailor your research descriptions to emphasize what your target discipline values most.

## Summary

Research experience descriptions communicate your scholarly contributions through structured entries with project titles, roles, and quantified outcomes. Use 3 to 5 bullets per project starting with strong action verbs. Specify methods and results with numbers whenever possible. Describe collaborative work with language that clarifies your individual contribution. Report negative results that represent substantial effort. Adapt formatting to your discipline's expectations for methods, outcomes, and interpretive contributions.
