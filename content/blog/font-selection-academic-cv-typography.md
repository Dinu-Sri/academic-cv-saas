Font selection affects how search committees perceive your academic CV before they read a single word. The right font choices convey professionalism, readability, and attention to typographic detail — qualities committees associate with scholarly competence. The wrong choices convey carelessness, outdated practices, or poor judgment. Typography matters because it shapes the reader's first impression of your document and, by extension, of you as a candidate.

Academic CV typography follows 3 principles: readability for extended reading, professional restraint over decorative display, and consistency throughout the document. A CV that uses 4 different fonts, mixes serif and sans-serif without purpose, or employs novelty typefaces signals typographic illiteracy. A CV that uses a clean, well-chosen font pairing with consistent hierarchy signals professionalism.

## Should You Use Serif or Sans-Serif Fonts for an Academic CV?

Serif fonts — those with small decorative strokes at the ends of letterforms — are traditional for academic documents because they improve readability in printed text. The serifs guide the eye along lines of text, reducing reading fatigue during extended review. Sans-serif fonts — those without serifs — provide cleaner appearance on screens and are increasingly common in academic CVs as committees shift toward digital review. Both choices are acceptable. The key is consistency, not the specific choice.

Recommended serif fonts include Garamond, Caslon, and Georgia for a traditional academic appearance. Computer Modern — the default LaTeX font — signals familiarity with scholarly typesetting conventions. Recommended sans-serif fonts include Helvetica, Calibri, and Source Sans Pro for a clean, modern appearance. Avoid Times New Roman — its overuse in default word processor templates makes documents appear generic. Avoid decorative, display, or handwriting fonts entirely.

## What Font Sizes Should You Use on an Academic CV?

Use 11-point or 12-point font for body text. Section headings should be 13-point or 14-point. Your name at the top of the CV may be 16-point to 18-point for visual hierarchy. Fonts smaller than 10-point strain readability, particularly for committee members reviewing documents on screens in their 50s and 60s. Fonts larger than 12-point consume excessive space and may signal that you are padding a thin CV.

For CVs that push against page limits, 10.5-point body text is the smallest acceptable size. For senior faculty with extensive records, 10-point body text is acceptable when paired with generous line spacing. Never reduce font size below 10-point. Search committee members will not magnify your document to read it — they will simply set it aside.
