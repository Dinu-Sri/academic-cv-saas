The skills section of an academic CV documents the technical, linguistic, and methodological competencies that enable your research. Search committees read this section to assess whether you possess the specific capabilities required for a position — whether you can operate the instrumentation in their laboratory, teach the methods courses in their curriculum, or conduct fieldwork in the languages relevant to their region of study.

A well-organized skills section groups competencies into logical categories with clear proficiency indicators. "Laboratory techniques" without subcategories or proficiency levels communicates less than "Molecular Biology: PCR, western blotting, CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing (advanced). Cell Culture: mammalian cell lines, primary neuron culture (advanced). Microscopy: confocal, two-photon, super-resolution (intermediate)." The second version provides actionable information. The first provides a list of keywords.

## How Should You Organize the Skills Section?

Organize skills into 4 to 6 categories based on your discipline. STEM researchers typically use categories such as laboratory techniques, analytical instrumentation, computational methods, programming languages, and statistical analysis. Social scientists may include research methodologies, statistical software, qualitative analysis methods, and language skills. Humanities scholars emphasize languages, archival research methods, digital humanities tools, and pedagogical techniques.

Within each category, list skills from most to least advanced. Use consistent proficiency indicators across all categories: "advanced," "intermediate," "basic," or "proficient," "familiar," "learning." Choose one indicator system and apply it throughout. For technical skills where proficiency is binary — you either can operate the instrument independently or you cannot — use checkmark indicators or omit proficiency labels entirely.

## How Should You Present Language Skills?

Language skills require 3 elements: the language name, the proficiency level, and the domain of proficiency (reading, writing, speaking, or translation). "French: reading knowledge (advanced), speaking (intermediate), writing (basic)" communicates specific capabilities that a generic "French: intermediate" does not.

For ancient and research languages, distinguish between active use and passive knowledge. "Latin: reading knowledge of classical and medieval Latin. Ancient Greek: reading knowledge of Attic and Koine Greek." These distinctions matter for classics, medieval studies, and theology positions where specific textual competencies are required. For modern languages, indicate whether you can conduct research, teach, or publish in the language. "Spanish: fluent in all domains. Can teach undergraduate courses and conduct fieldwork interviews in Spanish."

## How Should You Present Computational and Technical Skills?

Computational skills require specificity about languages, packages, and applications. "Python" is less informative than "Python (pandas, NumPy, scikit-learn, TensorFlow, matplotlib)." The package-level detail tells hiring committees whether your Python skills match their specific needs — a lab using TensorFlow for deep learning has different requirements than one using pandas for data cleaning.

Include version information for rapidly evolving tools only if the version affects capability. "R (tidyverse, lme4, brms)" is more useful than "R version 4.2.1" because the package ecosystem defines capability more than the base version. For specialized instruments, include manufacturer and model when the specific instrument matters: "X-ray Diffractometer (Rigaku Ultima IV), NMR Spectrometer (Bruker 600 MHz with cryoprobe)."

## How Should the Skills Section Change Across Career Stages?

The skills section contracts as your career advances and your publication record expands. A PhD student or postdoc may have a skills section occupying half a page. A senior faculty member may reduce the section to a few lines focused on the most advanced or unusual skills relevant to their current research. This contraction reflects the shift from skills-based evaluation (what can you do?) to accomplishment-based evaluation (what have you done with those skills?).

The skills section serves different purposes at different stages. For graduate school and postdoc applications, it demonstrates readiness to begin productive work immediately. For faculty positions, it demonstrates capability to teach specific methods courses and supervise students using specific techniques. For senior positions, it provides context for research accomplishments rather than standing as an independent qualification.

## Summary

Organize the skills section into 4 to 6 logical categories with consistent proficiency indicators. Present language skills with domain-specific proficiency levels. Detail computational skills at the package and tool level. Distinguish active from passive language knowledge for ancient and research languages. Adjust section length across career stages: detailed for early career, focused on advanced capabilities for senior scholars.
